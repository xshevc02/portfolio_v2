What I do at Thermo Fisher without the screens.

At Thermo Fisher Scientific, I work on high-tech B2B software used by scientists and engineers to control advanced electron microscopes.

Due to non-disclosure agreements, specific UI elements, proprietary algorithm parameters, and real scientific data stay private. This page shows the methods, the collaboration model, and the kinds of product decisions I make.

Who I work with

Cross-functional planning before pixels.

Designing for scientific instrumentation requires tight integration with both business goals and technical realities. Most of my design decisions are shaped in conversation before anything is drawn.

Application Specialists Internal scientists who operate and test microscopes in real material-testing scenarios. Their feedback refines interaction models and catches assumptions that would not hold in expert workflows.
Product Owners Quarterly planning, UX requirements, and feature readiness before implementation starts. Co-running usability sessions.
UX designer Me translating domain logic into workflows people can trust
Engineering Leads & developers Edge cases, technical constraints, and state transitions discussed early in the design phase, so handoff is shared understanding rather than static mockups.
Adjacent teams I act as an internal UX advisor, giving design reviews and recommendations when new functionality is initiated, to keep the wider product ecosystem consistent.

2

How a quarter runs

From roadmap to handoff, then back again.

  1. 1 → Planning Quarterly sessions with Product Owners and Engineering Leads to scope features and agree what is ready.
  2. 2 → Requirements UX requirements written before implementation, checked against domain logic with Application Specialists.
  3. 3 → Prototype Interaction models and high-fidelity prototypes aligned with the corporate design system.
  4. 4 → Testing Usability sessions with domain specialists, findings synthesized and presented to stakeholders.
  5. 5 ↺ Handoff Component documentation and handoff notes; what we learn feeds the next planning round.

3

One project, abstracted

Iterating on a core system workflow.

One of my primary initiatives was a multi-month iterative design process for a core system workflow. The question underneath: how can new capabilities fit into existing application structures without cluttering the interface or breaking the mental models expert users already rely on?

Phase 1

Requirements and brainstorming

Understanding what the new capabilities had to do and where they could live in the existing structure.

Phase 2

End-to-end journey mapping

Mapping user journeys across parameter-heavy system workflows.

Phase 3

Interaction logic

Defining how complex parameter inputs and system state changes behave.

Phase 4

High-fidelity prototypes

Translating those decisions into prototypes testable with real domain specialists.

Phase 5

Usability testing

Sessions with domain specialists to find points of confusion and validate assumptions.

Phase 6 · repeat

Iterate and present findings

Feedback turned into concrete design iterations, findings presented to stakeholders.
